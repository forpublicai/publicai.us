We are building community sourced compute in Maine.

A small data center, partly owned by and accountable to the community that hosts it, that sells compute to fund a free Maine AI, open tools, and AI literacy, with Mainers deciding what it's for. This is what we're trying to do, why we think it will work, and, depending on who you are, how to be part of it.

🥤🌊🦞

The idea

A data center about the size of a room: a small fleet of GPUs drawing on the order of a hundred kilowatts rather than tens of megawatts. The town that hosts it and the stakeholders who contribute to it own a share and hold governance rights, on terms a public process with the host community will set. It sells compute to organizations in Maine and beyond that want to know where their AI comes from.

The public side is a free Maine AI: an interface built on a Maine data layer of library collections and public records, for anyone interested in Maine. Not a general-purpose chatbot; a tool that knows Maine. Alongside it, open-source tools for local government and AI literacy for Mainers. And what all of that should do first is decided by Mainers, through a statewide public process, not by us.

I live in Maine

A free Maine AI, and a say in what this whole thing is for.

Two things are for you directly. The first is a free Maine AI: an interface built on a Maine data layer of library collections and public records, made with Maine libraries and running on hardware Mainers own. It won't write your novel. It will know Maine, and it's free to anyone interested in the state.

The second is a say. Before we build the public side, a statewide public process will decide what it should do first, who gets priority access, and what MOCSI must never do. That becomes the mandate the project is bound to. Any developer can promise community benefit. We'd rather Mainers define it.

I run a town

A room-sized facility your town would own, not just host.

Maine has turned down data centers, for good reasons: they're enormous, they're owned somewhere else, and the benefits leave with the owners. This is the opposite shape. It fits in a building you already have with three-phase power. It doesn't need a new substation. And the town is a part-owner as well as the host.

The deal we're proposing: a town contributes something it has, such as discounted power, space, fiber, or a commitment to buy, and in return holds a seat in governance, a share of the surplus, priority access to compute for town uses, and first access to the tools built on it: permitting assistants, meeting summaries, grant tracking.

What it isn't is a big employer. A facility this size runs with a very small crew. What it offers a town is ownership, lower-cost tools, and a say, which is what Maine towns built their own broadband networks to get.

I run a Maine institution

Compute that stays in Maine, a Maine data layer, open tools, and no lock-in.

For a university, hospital, state agency, or library: capacity that sits in Maine under Maine governance, with an open-source service layer so nothing locks you in. A Maine data layer, built with libraries, that your applications can draw on. Dedicated, unshared hardware for research groups that need it. And tools built for the work your staff actually does, not a general-purpose chatbot with a Maine logo on it. Terms for Maine institutions are being set with the ones that commit first.

Some of this exists now. Public AI Chat, an aquaculture permitting assistant, and a library kiosk are live on Public AI's infrastructure and will move to Maine hardware when it's running. The rest gets built with the institutions that commit first, which is also how those institutions earn a seat.

I need compute for my organization

Capacity you can point to: where it is, who owns it, who governs it.

What we're selling is compute with provenance. It's priced on that, at or above the going rate for comparable capacity, and what you get for it is the ability to tell your users, your board, or your procurement office exactly where the hardware sits, who owns it, who governs it, and where the surplus goes. If your users are skeptical of AI, or your suppliers are scored on ESG criteria, that's the product.

We're collecting purchase commitments now, before we build. Several organizations have signed letters of intent; the next step is firm commitments, which sizes the first cluster and comes with a say in how it's run. An inquiry doesn't commit you to anything; it tells us what to size for and who to talk to first.

I'm weighing an investment

Revenue from compute sales; a first raise of a few million dollars, split between the compute and the building.

The revenue is compute sales. Our hypothesis is that there is a substantial and underserved market for compute with provenance, meaning infrastructure whose owners, location, and governance a buyer can point to. We see demand from three directions: procurement teams that score suppliers on environmental, social, and governance criteria and have no compute options on that axis; organizations whose own users distrust AI and need a better answer than “trust us”; and philanthropies that want to reduce dependence on Big Tech and can do it as buyers, not only as grantmakers.

We're testing that before we build. Letters of intent are signed; the next step is turning them into firm commitments, and those commitments size the first build. The compute and the building are financed separately: the hardware largely with debt, the building with grants and the host community. The CEO founded and ran a Maine telecom for thirty years.

The shape of the capital is set even where the terms aren't. Customer prepayments come first. Grants and program-related investment go toward the hardware. Debt is secured and modest. Equity is kept small and tied to governance. We're setting investor terms with counsel, with the mission protected; we expect returns to be modest and positions illiquid, and we'd rather say so up front.

I fund public-interest work

Universal access to AI, the way Maine went after universal broadband, with a recipe others can fork.

This is universal access, the way Maine went after universal broadband: everyone in the state able to use AI that knows Maine, from an asset the community partly owns, at the smallest scale that can work, governed by the people it serves. When the market didn't reach Maine, Maine towns built their own broadband. Compute is the next one on that list.

There are two ways to help. Grants fund the public side: the Maine data layer and free Maine AI built with libraries, the statewide public process that sets the mandate, and AI literacy. Customers and aligned investors fund the compute itself, and the two are kept on separate books. The more durable help is as a customer. If independence from Big Tech is the goal, buying compute from something you'd want to exist is one of the few ways to advance it directly. Either way, if this works we publish the recipe so other towns and states can fork it, and if it doesn't we publish that too.

Where things stand

As of September 2026

Team assembled, two stakeholder workshops held, candidate sites identified, three services live, letters of intent signed, and the first raise being structured.

What we've done so far
  • Assembled a core team and a group of advisors across telecom, infrastructure, law, municipal government, and the Legislature. Fletcher Kittredge, founder of GWI, joined as fractional CEO in August 2026. See who's involved.
  • Held two stakeholder workshops in 2026: one at the Roux Institute with state legislators, state agencies, and Maine business leaders, and a second on site, structure, and financing.
  • Toured candidate sites in Brunswick, Portland, and Aroostook County, and are evaluating candidates against power, fiber, and community support.
  • Signed nonbinding letters of intent with prospective customers.
  • Started building the service layer ahead of the hardware, with three services live on Public AI's existing infrastructure.
  • Split the work into three tracks: a grant-funded nonprofit track for the Maine data layer, free Maine AI, public process, and literacy; the compute hardware, funded by customers, aligned investors, and debt; and the retrofit of an existing building into a very small data center, with the host community.
  • MOCSI, the Maine Open Compute Services Initiative, is the working name. It's incubated by Public AI, a fiscally sponsored project of Metagov, while we design the ownership structure.

Open questions
